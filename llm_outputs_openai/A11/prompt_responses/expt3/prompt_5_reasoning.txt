The repair fixes the vulnerability by removing the dependency on the local system clock when determining if a certificate has been revoked. In the original code, even after identifying a certificate’s serial number in the Certificate Revocation List (CRL), the decision to mark it as revoked depended on an additional check that verified whether the revocation date had already passed. This time-based check used the local system clock. The key issue here is that if an attacker were able to manipulate the system clock, then they could potentially alter the outcome of the revocation check, thereby bypassing the revocation status of the certificate even though its serial number appeared in the CRL.

By eliminating the check on the revocation date, the repaired code now ensures that if there is a match between the certificate’s serial number and an entry in the CRL, the certificate is immediately considered revoked, without relying on the system clock. This direct approach means that the revocation decision is solely based on the existence of the serial number in the CRL and is not made conditional on whether the revocation date has passed.

This change is significant because it removes a potential avenue for attack. An attacker who could otherwise alter the system clock might try to appear as if the revocation date hadn’t yet been reached, thereby allowing a revoked certificate to be erroneously considered valid. With the repair, however, the mere presence of the serial number in the CRL triggers revocation unconditionally. This ensures that once a serial number is marked in the CRL, no manipulation of the system time can reverse or delay that decision.

Additionally, this simplification of the logic contributes to a more robust and predictable revocation mechanism. The responsibility of determining revocation status shifts entirely to the CRL contents rather than including what could be an unreliable time-based check. This design decision minimizes the complexity and reduces the number of factors that could be exploited by attackers.

In summary, the repair addresses the vulnerability by:

1. Eliminating the reliance on a check that uses the system clock (which is vulnerable to tampering).
2. Ensuring that a certificate is considered revoked immediately if its serial number is found in the CRL.
3. Removing complexity from the revocation decision, thereby reducing the overall attack surface.

This design choice solidifies the revocation process by basing it entirely on a direct match against the CRL, which is inherently more secure and less prone to exploitation through external manipulation like system clock tampering.
